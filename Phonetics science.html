Phonetics & Speech Science: Comprehensive Guide

A deep dive into the physiological production, physical transmission, and digital representation of human speech sounds.

The Foundations of Phonetics

Taxonomy of Speech Science

Phonetics is defined as the physical study of speech sounds. It focuses on the concrete, measurable aspects of human communication rather than abstract systems. The field is traditionally divided into three distinct branches based on the "Speech Chain":

  • Articulatory Phonetics: The study of how the human vocal tract produces speech sounds. This is considered the most primitive and foundational branch, as it maps physical movements to sound outcomes.
  • Acoustic Phonetics: The analysis of the physical properties of speech as it transmits through the air in the form of sound waves.
  • Auditory Phonetics: The study of how the ear receives these sound waves and how the brain decodes them into meaningful units.
Historical Background: While early phonetics relied on visual observation of the articulators, modern phonetics utilizes advanced medical imaging (like MRI) and complex signal processing to analyze the "hidden" movements of the tongue and vocal folds.

A key concept here is the Phoneme: a temporary sound unit that we hear and interpret as a distinct signal in our mental lexicon.

Biological Mechanics of Production

The Vocal Tract and Laryngeal Function

Speech is the result of air being pushed from the lungs and manipulated by various Articulators (or constrictors). The primary pathway for this air is the vocal tract, which includes the oral, nasal, and pharyngeal cavities.

  • The Epiglottis: A vital structure that acts as a valve, opening and closing during swallowing to protect the airway. In phonetics, we focus on the vocal folds located nearby; during the production of certain sounds, these folds may vibrate or "clasp" and release up to 440 times per second.
  • Voicing vs. Whispering: Voiced sounds (like vowels) occur when the vocal folds vibrate against each other. In contrast, during "whispering," the vocal folds are held apart so they do not vibrate, resulting in a turbulent, voiceless airflow.
  • Nasalization (ㄴ, ㅁ): These sounds are produced when the oral passage is blocked (e.g., by the lips for /m/ or tongue for /n/), but the nasal passage remains open, allowing air to escape through the nose.

Acoustic Analysis and Spectroscopy

Pitch, Frequency, and Transformation

Sound waves are complex, but they can be broken down into simpler components. The Sine Wave represents the most basic shape of a sound wave.

Frequency and Pitch:

  • Pitch is the perceptual correlate of frequency, measured in hertz (Hz).
  • The unit for measuring how often the glottal structures close is the fundamental frequency ($f_0$).
  • A "high" pitch corresponds to a greater number of vibrations per second.

Signal Processing:

  • Fast Fourier Transform (FFT): A mathematical algorithm used to identify specific frequency components in a sound.
  • Spectrogram: A visual representation that combines multiple frequency spectra over time. It shows energy (darkness) across frequency (y-axis) and time (x-axis).

The Vowel Space and Resonances

The Source-Filter Model

Vowel production is best understood through the Source-Filter Theory. The "Source" is the vocal fold vibration, while the "Filter" is the vocal tract above the larynx.

  • The physical specificity of the vocal tract determines its Resonance—certain frequencies are amplified based on the shape of the oral cavity.
  • Formants: These are the resonant frequencies of the vocal tract, appearing as dark horizontal bands on a spectrogram. Usually, only F1 and F2 are required to identify a vowel.
F1 (First Formant): Corresponds inversely to vowel height. High vowels (like /i/) have a low F1.
F2 (Second Formant): Corresponds to vowel backness. Front vowels (like /i/) have a high F2.

The Schwa (/ə/): This vowel is produced with the most neutral, uniform tube-like configuration of the vocal tract. Its resonant frequencies are typically around 500Hz and 1500Hz.

Digital Signal Processing (DSP)

Digitizing the Human Voice

To analyze speech on a computer, the analog sound pressure must be converted into digital data.

  • Sampling Rate: Measured in Hz, this defines how many snapshots of the sound wave are taken per second.
  • Bit Rate: Represents the total amount of digital information processed per second.
  • Standing Waves: Within the vocal tract, speech sounds are often the result of waves colliding with their own reflections. This interference creates "standing waves," which are the physical basis for resonance and formants.
